# Study notes for the generated image layer

Before writing any prompt we looked at well-regarded works that show each technique clearly. The prompts take craft lessons from them: light, composition, and what makes the device read. They do not copy any work's composition or subject, name a living artist, or imitate an identifiable style. Definitions come from `atlas/data/taxonomy.json`.

## Symmetry and central balance (`symmetry`)

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Marriage of the Virgin (Lo Sposalizio) | Raphael | 1504 | https://pinacotecabrera.org/en/collezioni/collezione-on-line/the-marriage-of-the-virgin/ |
| The Ideal City (Urbino panel) | Formerly attributed to Piero della Francesca | c. 1480 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ideal_City_(painting) |

Lessons:
- One structure sits exactly on the vertical axis, and the paving or architecture runs to it. The axis is drawn by the whole picture, not only by the subject.
- The two halves balance in mass and tone, not only in outline. A dark mass on one side and a light one on the other breaks the mirror.
- Even, frontal light, with no raking shadow across the axis, keeps the halves equal.
- Small departures (figures placed differently on each side) make the order feel alive without breaking it.
- A water reflection gives a second, horizontal axis for free. A dead-calm surface is the condition.

## Unusual vantage point (`vantage-point`)

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Berlin Radio Tower (From the Radio Tower, Berlin) | László Moholy-Nagy | 1928 | https://www.artic.edu/artworks/55476/berlin-radio-tower |
| The Boulevard Seen from Above | Gustave Caillebotte | 1880 | https://nga.gov/features/slideshows/gustave-caillebotte.html |

Lessons:
- Pointing the camera nearly straight down removes the horizon, and the ground becomes a flat graphic map. Paths, shadows and small figures turn into pattern.
- Low, raking light (snow or wet ground in Moholy-Nagy's view) makes the ground plane legible from above, and long shadows show that the picture was taken from overhead.
- Caillebotte tips the street up into the picture plane. A tree, bench and passers-by seen from the top are foreshortened to discs and dots, and this is what tells the viewer how high they are.
- The viewpoint has to be extreme to read. A slightly raised view looks like a normal view.

## Proximity grouping (`proximity-grouping`)

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Harvesters | Pieter Bruegel the Elder | 1565 | https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/435809 |
| A Sunday on La Grande Jatte — 1884 | Georges Seurat | 1884–86 | https://www.artic.edu/artworks/27992/a-sunday-on-la-grande-jatte-1884 |

Lessons:
- Bruegel clusters the resting reapers tightly under the pear tree and sets single workers apart in the wheat. The gaps tell you who belongs together before you read any gesture.
- The clusters work because the figures look alike. If similarity varied as much as spacing, the groups would break apart (see the taxonomy's limitation).
- Seurat sets groups on an open, even ground (lawn, water), so the empty intervals are as readable as the figures.
- One figure set clearly apart from the groups is the strongest cue. Give it the same size and tone as the others, so distance alone isolates it.

## Figure–ground ambiguity (`figure-ground-ambiguity`)

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sky and Water I | M. C. Escher | 1938 | https://www.nga.gov/artworks/54215-sky-and-water-i |
| Rubin's vase (figure–ground demonstrations) | Edgar Rubin | 1915 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubin_vase |

Lessons:
- Only two flat tones, with no modelling, shading or texture on either side of the contour. Any shading makes one side the figure.
- The two tones need about equal area, and the contour they share has to be meaningful from both sides.
- Escher lets the flip happen in a central band and resolves it at the edges. The ambiguous zone is where the eye works.
- Rubin's demonstrations show that a symmetric, centred shape with recognisable outlines on its outer side (profiles) flips most reliably.
- A graphic medium (cut paper, woodcut, screenprint) suits the device. A photograph tends to resolve it.

## Selective focus (`selective-focus`)

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Lacemaker | Johannes Vermeer | c. 1669–70 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lacemaker_(Vermeer) |
| Kate Keown | Julia Margaret Cameron | 1866 | https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/286404 |

Lessons:
- Vermeer paints the threads in the foreground as soft, almost abstract blobs, while the hands and bobbins are crisp. Blur in front of the subject as well as behind it places the plane of focus exactly.
- In painting, the same effect comes from handling: tight, small strokes on the subject and loose, broad strokes elsewhere.
- Cameron's soft focus comes from a shallow plane, long exposures and gentle side light. Softness reads as atmosphere, and the one sharp area pulls the eye.
- The subject keeps the same light and colour as its surroundings, and only sharpness separates it. This is what distinguishes selective focus from value contrast.
- Blur should be smooth and optically plausible: discs of light in the background, with no cut-out edges around the sharp subject.

## Leading lines (`leading-lines`), batch 2

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Avenue at Middelharnis | Meindert Hobbema | 1689 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Avenue_at_Middelharnis |
| Paris Street; Rainy Day | Gustave Caillebotte | 1877 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Street;_Rainy_Day |

Lessons:
- A road or path that starts at the bottom edge and runs to one point carries the eye through the whole depth; whatever sits at that point is read last and remembered.
- Rails, kerbs and rows repeat the same direction, so several weak lines make one strong pull.

## Tight cropping (`tight-cropping`), batch 2

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Child's Bath | Mary Cassatt | 1893 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Child%27s_Bath |

Lessons:
- A close, high view cut by the frame edges puts the viewer inside the moment; nothing outside it competes.
- What leaves the frame is implied, not lost: the eye completes the bowl, the boat, the scene.

## Gaze direction (`gaze-direction`), batch 2

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young Man at His Window | Gustave Caillebotte | 1876 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Man_at_His_Window |
| Girl with a Pearl Earring | Johannes Vermeer | c. 1665 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girl_with_a_Pearl_Earring |

Lessons:
- A figure looking somewhere makes the viewer look there too; leave room in that direction.
- A turned head and sideways eyes make a question; the target can stay out of the frame.

## Contextual incongruity (`contextual-incongruity`), batch 2

| Work | Creator | Date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Song of Love | Giorgio de Chirico | 1914 | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Song_of_Love |

Lessons:
- Ordinary objects, each shown plainly and correctly, placed where they do not belong. The mismatch alone makes the second look; nothing needs to be strange in itself.
- Studied for the device only; the generated pictures use different objects, places and light.
